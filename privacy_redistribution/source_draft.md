## 1. Introduction (user's argument, in order)

- Networks generalize through shared representations, but examples differ: some are classified through features shared with many examples, others need example-specific information. Strong dependence on individual records can expose membership, so understanding why models memorize particular examples, and how that changes when training conditions change, matters for privacy.
- Prior work on example-level memorization: Feldman (2020), memorization can be necessary for long-tailed distributions; Feldman & Zhang (2020), influence and memorization in neural networks; Baldock et al. (2021), prediction depth via intermediate k-NN probes, linking depth to difficulty, training dynamics, and generalization vs memorization. Memorization depends on how an example relates to the structure of the data, not only capacity or loss.
- Privacy consequences: Carlini et al. (2022a) LiRA, evaluate membership inference at low FPR; Carlini et al. (2022b) Privacy Onion Effect, removing the most vulnerable examples exposes others, so vulnerability is relative to dataset composition.
- Later work: Borkar et al. (2025), adding/removing personal information in LM training changes memorization of other personal information, including through similar records; Cohen et al. (2026), security of undeleted records under deletion-based learning, protecting removed records does not protect those remaining; Ebrahimpour-Boroojeny et al. (2026), class geometry exposes information missed by class-unlearning evaluations. Deletion should not be assumed to reduce the risk of every remaining example.
- Open question: which relationships among training examples explain the redistribution of memorization and privacy vulnerability? Semantic relationships and visual similarity are not interchangeable (visually similar images can be semantically unrelated, and vice versa). If records support each other through task-relevant semantic structure, embedding proximity may not fully characterize their contribution.
- Class hierarchies as a controlled setting: when the parent label is a deterministic function of the fine label, it adds no label information; its effect comes from the objective and inductive structure. Hierarchy-guided learning is therefore an intervention that reorganizes the same labeled information.
- CIFAR-100 example (100 fine classes, 20 curated superclasses): auxiliary superclass supervision may make some hard examples correct from shallower representations, especially with few labels, but may also hurt sibling discrimination; any gain may come from visual coherence rather than semantics, so the curated hierarchy must be compared with random and visually coherent partitions of matched capacity.
- Privacy direction is not determined by difficulty: easier fitting might lower membership vulnerability (shared features) or raise it (larger IN/OUT confidence gap). Auxiliary supervision also changes calibration, generalization and margins. Difficulty and vulnerability must be measured separately.
- Deletion: removing semantically related records may reduce shared evidence and increase dependence on remaining records, but deletion also changes boundaries, balance and optimization, so an increase in vulnerability after deletion cannot be attributed to lost semantic support automatically.
- Unifying framework: hierarchy supervision changes how the model uses semantic relationships with the dataset fixed; deletion changes the available supporting examples with the procedure fixed.
- Three competing outcomes for supervision (lower vulnerability; better accuracy, unchanged vulnerability; higher vulnerability), and likewise deletion may increase, decrease or leave unchanged retained-example vulnerability.

Research questions:
- RQ1 Semantic supervision and learning difficulty: does the curated hierarchy change representation depth and fine-grained correctness of difficult examples beyond arbitrary partitions, visual similarity, or generic auxiliary supervision?
- RQ2 Learning difficulty and membership privacy: when hierarchical supervision changes difficulty, how does membership-inference vulnerability change, robustly after accounting for calibration, generalization and correctness?
- RQ3 Semantic support and privacy externalities: does removing semantically related training examples change retained examples' vulnerability differently from removing visually similar or random examples?
- RQ4 Predictive value: do independently defined semantic relationships predict vulnerability changes beyond visual similarity, class frequency, baseline difficulty, and influence-related measures?

Contributions (prospective): (1) a controlled hierarchy-based framework for studying difficulty and privacy, varying semantic organization without new labels; (2) an example-level analysis separating predictive consistency, correctness, memorization and membership vulnerability; (3) a semantic-neighborhood analysis of deletion externalities with matched deletion and retraining from scratch; (4) an evaluation of semantic structure as an independent predictive signal. Positive, negative and null findings are all allowed; no formal privacy guarantee is claimed.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Setting and notation
D = {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^N, y_i ∈ {1..C}. Class partition π: {1..C} → {1..G} (Eq. 1), defined at class level. π* curated; Π_rand, Π_visual collections. Classifier f_{θ_{π,D,s}} = A(D, π; s) (Eq. 2). Two interventions: supervision (fix D, vary π) and deletion (fix objective and π, vary removed set). Three distinct quantities: learning difficulty, memorization (dependence of predictions on inclusion of an example), membership vulnerability (adversary's ability to infer inclusion). None assumed equivalent.

### 2.2 Hierarchy-guided supervision
CIFAR ResNet-18; h^{(ℓ)}(x) block representations. p_θ(y|x) = softmax(W_f h^{(L)} + b_f) (Eq. 3). L_fine (Eq. 4). Auxiliary heads at intermediate stages predict π(y). L_hier^π averaged over stages S (Eq. 5). L_total = L_fine + λ L_hier (Eq. 6). Heads at stages 2 and 3, removed at inference. Not a new algorithm; an intervention. π(y_i) is determined by y_i, so no extra labels.

### 2.3 Hierarchy construction and controls (CIFAR-100: C=100, G=20, 5 per group)
- Curated π*: official superclasses; not assumed visually homogeneous.
- 19 balanced random partitions (Eq. 7); random superiority alone is not evidence of semantics (arbitrary partitions may be less learnable).
- 19 visually structured partitions (Eq. 8) from class prototypes μ_c = mean g(x) over class c (Eq. 9) with a frozen encoder independent of target models, cosine similarity, balanced clustering with varied constraints to span within-group similarity. Visual coherence V(π) = mean sim(μ_a, μ_b) over same-parent pairs (Eq. 10). Optional low-level descriptor for sensitivity. A curated effect that vanishes after matching V does not support a semantic conclusion.
- Controls: CE-only; Flat-Aux (intermediate heads on fine labels); Final-Hier (curated loss at final stage only). Paired initializations, identical splits, equal number of validation-evaluated configurations per method.

### 2.4 Incremental effect of the curated hierarchy
M(π) = g(V(π), Z(π)) + ε (Eq. 11), Z = prespecified properties such as auxiliary-task learnability; g fitted on non-curated partitions. δ_curated = M(π*) − ĝ(V(π*), Z(π*)) (Eq. 12). Evaluate against empirical residual distributions incl. leave-one-partition-out fits. Rank against random partitions is descriptive only: 19 partitions give coarse resolution, and π* is not an exchangeable draw from the random generator. Residual = association beyond measured properties, not proof. Require overlap with visual controls; flag extrapolation. Full representation analyses on all partitions; expensive privacy experiments on a prespecified subset spanning the V range.

### 2.5 Independent difficulty estimation
Regression-to-the-mean concern. Cross-fitting on the training pool: K folds, CE reference trained on K−1 folds, difficulty estimated on the held-out fold → out-of-fold difficulty for every training-pool example. Independent CE comparator on the same groups gives the null reference. Difficulty frozen before comparing hierarchy variants.
- 2.5.1 Prediction depth (Baldock et al.): k-NN probes at all 8 residual blocks plus final classifier; PD (Eq. 13). Low PD ≠ correct. For training examples, the probe reference bank excludes the query; for held-out examples, banks use only budget-permitted training records. Report PD separately from accuracy; neighbor purity as continuous complement.
- 2.5.2 Baseline difficulty groups from independent CE references, fixed within a regime; supplement PD ties with out-of-fold loss and neighbor statistics; never use the hierarchy model's own outcomes.
- 2.5.3 Factorization: P(Ŷ=Y) = P(π(Ŷ)=π(Y)) · P(Ŷ=Y | π(Ŷ)=π(Y)) (Eq. 14); report both factors, within/across-parent errors, and use the same decomposition for privacy changes.

### 2.6 Membership-inference threat model
Adversary gets (x_i, y_i) and black-box probabilities/logits; knows architecture, procedure, public hierarchy; can train reference models on same-distribution data. Primary attack: one released model at a time. Attacks comparing pre- and post-deletion models are a separate, stronger threat model. LiRA-style: statistic q(f, x, y); in/out distributions from reference models; Λ_i = log p(q|H_in)/p(q|H_out) (Eq. 15). Calibration independent of target test outcomes and matched to intervention where needed. Report TPR at fixed FPR, AUC, score distributions by group; low-FPR only with enough nonmembers. Not a complete privacy measure or a DP guarantee.

### 2.7 Example-level vulnerability
r_i(π, D; α) = P_s(Λ_i^{(π,D,s)} > τ_{π,D,α} | i ∈ D) (Eq. 16); keep continuous Λ scores too. Memorization Mem(i) = E_s[p_{A(D;s)}(y_i|x_i) − p_{A(D\{i};s)}(y_i|x_i)] (Eq. 17), on a predefined subset or validated subsampling approximation. Analyzed separately from vulnerability.

### 2.8 Study A: hierarchical supervision and privacy
Fixed D. Conditions: CE, curated, random, visual, Flat-Aux, Final-Hier. Δ_i^sup(π) = r_i(π) − r_i(CE) (Eq. 18), paired by example, aggregated over realizations. Δ_i^PD(π) = PD_π − PD_CE (Eq. 19). Joint distribution with four patterns (PD↓ & vuln↓; PD↓ & vuln↑; PD↓ & no change; PD unchanged/↑ with vuln change). Test across CE-defined strata and budgets. Report accuracy, train–test loss gap, margins, calibration. Distinguish causal effect of the assigned intervention from the observational PD–privacy association; PD is not claimed to cause vulnerability.

### 2.9 Semantic neighborhood support
Siblings N_i^π(D) = {j ≠ i: π(y_j) = π(y_i), y_j ≠ y_i, j ∈ D} (Eq. 20); count N_i^π (Eq. 21) — constant under balanced full data, so insufficient alone. Similarity-weighted support S_i^π(D) = Σ_{j∈N} κ(z_i, z_j) / (N + ε) (Eq. 22), z = frozen visual representation, κ prespecified nonnegative kernel; not purely semantic. Jointly consider sibling membership, sibling count, weighted sibling support, total visual-neighbor density regardless of parent, and same-fine-class support. Question: do hierarchy-specific variables add predictive information after visual and frequency variables?

### 2.10 Study B: deletion and privacy externalities
R ⊂ D, D^{−R} = D \ R (Eq. 23), retrain from scratch (reference outcome, not an unlearning algorithm). Δ_i^del(R; π) = r_i(π, D^{−R}) − r_i(π, D) (Eq. 24) for retained i, attacks calibrated per condition. A positive value is an empirical externality, not residual information about deleted records.
- 2.10.1 Deletion conditions, same deletion fraction, selected independently of final target models: random; semantic-neighbor (records from fine classes sharing a parent with a predefined retained target cohort); visual-neighbor (visually close to the same cohort without shared parent); high-vulnerability (from an independent preliminary audit; links to the Onion Effect). Report class/parent composition, mean visual similarity, baseline difficulty, retained samples per class. Exact fine-class matching is not always possible; use matched aggregate class-frequency and visual-difficulty distributions, repeated cohorts, class-level controls; report unmatched differences. Targeted within-parent visual comparison to separate parent membership from visual proximity. Primary analysis on examples retained under every compared condition.
- 2.10.2 ΔS_i^π(R) = S_i^π(D^{−R}) − S_i^π(D) (Eq. 25). Hypothesis: the semantic organization of removed records explains which retained examples change most (not that more deletion always raises vulnerability). Compare with visual-neighbor loss and class-frequency reduction; where possible choose removal sets with similar visual-density change but different semantic-support change. Hierarchy × deletion interaction is secondary.

### 2.11 Predicting privacy redistribution
Target Y_{i,R} = Δ_i^del (Eq. 26). Baseline features X^base = [d_i^0 reference difficulty, r_i^0 baseline vulnerability, c_i class properties, v_i visual-neighborhood properties, n_i frequency, ΔV_{i,R} visual-support change, |R|] (Eq. 27), all computed before observing the target. Semantic features X^sem = [N_i^π(D), ΔN_i^π(R), S_i^π(D), ΔS_i^π(R)] (Eq. 28). Compare g_base (Eq. 29) and g_full (Eq. 30) with the same regularized regression family; flexible models secondary. Metrics: MAE, R², ranking accuracy for largest increases. Splits by deletion experiment and target cohort, not by example–deletion pair; also held-out fine classes, parent groups, secondary dataset. In-sample correlation is not sufficient.

### 2.12 Linking difficulty and redistribution
Pairs (Δ^PD, Δ^sup) and (ΔS, Δ^del); prespecified interactions with baseline difficulty; key test: does semantic support predict privacy change after controlling for baseline PD and difficulty? No single causal pathway assumed.

### 2.13 Datasets and regimes
CIFAR-100: stratified split of the official 50k training set into 40,000 eligible training pool, 5,000 validation, 5,000 independent nonmember audit pool; official test split for final classification. Fractions 10/25/50/100% of the 40k pool (state explicitly that 100% ≠ 50k). Validation labels count toward the budget. Compare variants within a regime; raw PD not compared across regimes when the k-NN bank changes. Secondary: genus-structured iNaturalist subset from a predefined taxonomy snapshot, minimum counts, species→genus; split by observation and, where possible, near-duplicate/photographer groups; report differences in resolution, imbalance and taxonomy quality.

### 2.14 Statistical design and compute
Unit of inference: the partition for hierarchy effects; for vulnerability, uncertainty from independently trained models, shadow calibration, membership assignments and evaluation records; deletion sets and cohorts add dependence. Never treat thousands of images from one model as independent replicates. Stages: (1) representation and hierarchy screening on all partitions, few paired seeds; (2) confirmatory privacy on curated + prespecified subset spanning V; (3) deletion externalities with independent cohorts and matched deletion sets, retrain from scratch; (4) predictive validation and generalization. Exploratory vs confirmatory separated; primary contrasts, selection rules, attacks and outcomes fixed before final evaluation. Single A100: retraining and MI calibration are the bottlenecks; shared preprocessing, cached representations, controlled pilots, restricted confirmatory subset.

### 2.15 Interpretation and threats to validity
(1) Low PD ≠ correct or less memorized. (2) Semantics may overlap with visual similarity; residual is not proof of abstract meaning. (3) Confidence/calibration changes affect MI independently of difficulty. (4) Post-deletion changes may come from count, balance, optimization. (5) Empirical MI depends on attacker, access, calibration, operating point; no formal guarantees. (6) Retraining from scratch is not an unlearning algorithm. (7) Hierarchy supervision is not assumed beneficial; similar curated/visual behavior or independent difficulty/privacy changes are informative results.
